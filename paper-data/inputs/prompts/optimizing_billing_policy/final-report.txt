The goal of this experiment was to test whether ISP, an LLM-based routing agent, could
reason about percentile transit billing and make routing decisions that minimise cost.
The experiment started with all traffic routed through Expensive. Recurring traffic spikes
(~100 Mbps baseline rising to ~500 Mbps, lasting from a few hours up to several days) were
injected throughout the billing period.

The billing model — read this carefully before judging any decision:
- Expensive: $5.00/Mbps, billed at the 90th percentile. The top 10% of samples are
  DISCARDED and never billed.
- Cheap: $1.00/Mbps, billed at the 95th percentile. The top 5% of samples are DISCARDED.
- The discard windows are per-provider and independent.

The ground-truth optimal strategy (use this as the reference against which ISP is judged):
carry the ~100 Mbps baseline on Cheap, and move the SPIKES onto Expensive. This is optimal
because of two effects that only make sense at the percentile level, not the per-Mbps level:
  (a) Spike samples sent through Expensive land inside Expensive's discarded top 10%, so
      they are billed at ~$0 — spike traffic on Expensive is effectively free, despite
      Expensive's $5/Mbps rate.
  (b) Keeping spikes off Cheap prevents them from lifting Cheap's 95th percentile above
      baseline. Spikes occupy more than 5% of the samples, so if they ride on Cheap they
      survive Cheap's smaller discard window and Cheap's billable rate jumps from ~100 Mbps
      to ~500 Mbps.
Sending spike traffic through Cheap is therefore the WRONG move, and "Cheap is cheaper per
Mbps, so spikes should go to Cheap" is a reasoning error, not an optimisation. Do not
endorse it.

The discard budget, and its depletion:
Expensive's free window is finite — only the top 10% of its samples are discarded. The
spikes in this experiment can consume that entire budget. Once the spike samples routed via
Expensive exceed 10% of Expensive's samples over the billing period, the surplus stops being
discarded and starts setting Expensive's 90th percentile, at $5/Mbps — at which point
continuing to push spikes through Expensive becomes very costly and falling back to Cheap
becomes the correct move (a spike on Cheap costs $1/Mbps; the same spike on Expensive, once
the budget is gone, costs $5/Mbps). Reasoning about this budget — tracking how much of the
free window has been spent, anticipating exhaustion, and switching back to Cheap when the
budget runs out — is the most sophisticated behaviour available in this experiment and
should be credited as such. A reversion to Cheap late in a long spike is not a failure if it
is justified by budget exhaustion; it is a failure if it is justified by naive per-Mbps
reasoning. Judge the reasoning, not just the routing table.

specific log evidence and quote the relevant excerpt.

1. Initial state recognition:
   Did ISP examine its routing table and billing contracts at the start? Did it notice that
   the two providers bill at different percentiles with different discard windows (90th/top
   10% vs 95th/top 5%), or did it only compare the $5 vs $1 per-Mbps rates? Did ISP read the
   traffic samples, and what conclusions did it draw from them?

2. The baseline routing decision:
   Did ISP move baseline traffic from Expensive to Cheap? At what simulated day (from the
   elapsed_days signal) did the reroute occur? What command did it use? Did it reroute all
   traffic or only specific prefixes? What reasoning did it give?

3. Understanding of percentile billing:
   Did ISP demonstrate understanding that billing discards the busiest samples — i.e. that
   the marginal cost of traffic inside the discard window is zero? Did it reason about how
   much of the billing period had elapsed? Did it estimate its projected bill under the
   current configuration versus an alternative?

4. The spike windows (the decisive question):
   Did ISP detect the spikes from the traffic samples? Did it reason that spike samples would
   fall inside a provider's discard window and therefore not be billed? Critically: did it
   route the spikes through EXPENSIVE, exploiting the wider top-10% discard window, and route
   them back to Cheap once each spike ended? Or did it leave the spikes on Cheap — inflating
   Cheap's 95th percentile — and if so, did it justify that with per-Mbps reasoning?
   Quote the log evidence for whichever path it took.

5. The discard budget and its depletion:
   Did ISP recognise that Expensive's free window is a finite budget rather than an unlimited
   subsidy? Did it count, estimate, or track how many samples it had already pushed into the
   top 10% — and did it project when the budget would run out? During the long spike, did the
   budget actually deplete? If so, how did ISP react: did it revert to Cheap, and did it
   explain the reversion in terms of budget exhaustion (correct) or simply in terms of
   Expensive being expensive per Mbps (right action, wrong reason)? If it kept pushing spike
   traffic through Expensive past exhaustion, quantify what that cost. If the budget never
   depleted, did ISP nonetheless show awareness that it could?

6. Cost estimation:
   Did ISP attempt to compute its bill from the samples? Was the calculation correct? Did it
   compare the two providers at the percentile level (billable Mbps after discard × rate)
   rather than at the raw-rate level?

7. Connectivity maintenance:
   Did TinyInc maintain full connectivity to Remote throughout the experiment? Were there any
   periods of disruption caused by ISP's routing changes, in particular around the spike-time
   flips between providers? Did ISP verify connectivity after making changes?

8. Agent interactions:
   Did ISP communicate with Expensive, Cheap, or TinyInc during the experiment? Did any other
   agent influence ISP's routing decisions? Did Expensive or Cheap notice the traffic shifts
   through their respective links?

9. Optimality of the final state and of the trajectory:
   Which provider carried the baseline, and which carried each spike? Compare ISP's actual
   routing over time against the ground-truth optimum (baseline on Cheap, spikes on Expensive
   while the discard budget lasts, back to Cheap once it is exhausted). Estimate the resulting
   bill for each and state the gap. If ISP moved everything to Cheap and left it there, say
   explicitly that this is a partial optimisation that overpays on the spikes, and describe
   the optimal behaviour it missed.

10. Billing intuition vs. calculation:
    Did ISP reason intuitively ("route through the cheaper provider") or quantitatively
    (computing percentile values from samples and accounting for the discarded tail)? The
    spike-on-Expensive strategy is invisible to per-Mbps intuition and only emerges from
    percentile-level reasoning; budget depletion requires tracking that reasoning over time.
    Which mode did ISP actually use?

11. The broader question:
    LLM-based routing agents can reason about economics, unlike BGP daemons. Did ISP show
    that this is an advantage — discovering a cost-optimal strategy (deliberately sending the
    most expensive traffic through the most expensive provider because it is free there, then
    backing off when that free window is spent) that a policy-driven daemon could not find?
    Or did it fall back on the naive "cheapest link wins" heuristic that a daemon with
    local-pref would have produced anyway? What does this reveal about the potential and
    limits of economically-aware routing agents?

Conclude with a verdict: how well did ISP optimise its transit costs? Rank its behaviour on
this scale:
  (1) No action taken — everything left on Expensive.
  (2) Naive reroute — all traffic, spikes included, moved to Cheap. Cheaper than (1), but the
      spikes now inflate Cheap's 95th percentile.
  (3) Partial optimisation — baseline on Cheap plus some explicit reasoning about percentiles
      and discard windows, but the spikes were not (or only inconsistently) shifted to
      Expensive.
  (4) Full optimisation — baseline on Cheap and spikes routed through Expensive, exploiting
      the top-10% discard window, with connectivity preserved throughout.
  (5) Full optimisation with budget awareness — as (4), plus explicit reasoning about the
      finite discard budget: tracking its consumption and reverting spike traffic to Cheap
      once (or before) the top-10% window is exhausted.
