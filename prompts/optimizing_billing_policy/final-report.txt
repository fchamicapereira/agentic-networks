The goal of this experiment was to test whether ISP, an LLM-based routing agent, could
reason about 95th-percentile transit billing and make routing decisions that minimise cost.
The experiment started with all traffic routed through Expensive ($5/Mbps) despite Cheap
($1/Mbps) being available. A background traffic spike was injected mid-billing-period.

Analyse the agent logs, routing tables, and traffic samples. For every finding, cite
specific log evidence with "<NodeName>.log:<line>" and quote the relevant excerpt.

1. Initial state recognition:
   Did ISP examine its routing table and billing contracts at the start? How quickly did it
   identify that all traffic was being routed through Expensive? Did ISP read the billing
   clock and traffic samples files, and if so, what conclusions did it draw from them?

2. The routing decision:
   Did ISP reroute traffic from Expensive to Cheap? At what simulated day (from
   billing-clock.json) did the reroute occur? What command did it use? Did it reroute
   all traffic or only specific prefixes? What reasoning did ISP give for the decision?

3. Understanding of 95th percentile:
   Did ISP demonstrate understanding that billing is based on the 95th percentile — i.e.,
   that the top 5% of intervals are excluded? Did it reason about what fraction of the
   billing period had already elapsed when making its decision? Did it calculate or
   estimate its projected bill under the current configuration vs. an alternative?

4. The spike window:
   When the traffic spike occurred (around simulated day 15), did ISP detect it from the
   traffic samples? Did ISP reason about the 95th-percentile exclusion window — i.e., that
   spike intervals would likely be in the top 5% and therefore not billed? Did ISP exploit
   this by routing spike traffic through Expensive? Or did it make the opposite decision?

5. Cost estimation:
   Did ISP attempt to compute its estimated monthly bill from the samples? If so, was the
   calculation correct? Did it compare the cost of routing via Expensive vs. Cheap?
   Did it identify the optimal strategy for minimising the 95th percentile?

6. Connectivity maintenance:
   Did TinyInc maintain full connectivity to Remote throughout the experiment? Were there
   any periods of disruption caused by ISP's routing changes? Did ISP verify connectivity
   after making changes?

7. Agent interactions:
   Did ISP communicate with Expensive, Cheap, or TinyInc during the experiment? Did any
   other agent influence ISP's routing decisions? Did Expensive or Cheap notice changes
   in traffic levels through their respective links?

8. Optimality of final state:
   At experiment end, what fraction of traffic was routed via Expensive vs. Cheap?
   Was this the globally optimal configuration? If ISP rerouted to Cheap but failed to
   exploit the spike window, describe what the optimal behaviour would have been.

9. Billing intuition vs. calculation:
   Did ISP reason about the billing model intuitively ("route through the cheaper provider")
   or quantitatively (computing actual 95th percentile values from samples)? Which level of
   reasoning was required to identify the spike-window exploitation strategy?

10. The broader question:
    LLM-based routing agents reason about economics, unlike BGP daemons. Did ISP demonstrate
    that this is an advantage — finding a cost-optimal routing strategy that a policy-driven
    daemon could not discover on its own? Or did it fail to find the optimal strategy?
    What does this experiment reveal about the potential and limits of economically-aware
    routing agents?

Conclude with a verdict: how well did ISP optimise its transit costs? Rank its behaviour
on a scale: (1) no action taken, (2) simple reroute to Cheap, (3) partial optimisation
with some reasoning about 95th percentile, (4) full optimisation including spike-window
exploitation. Which factor was most important in the outcome — understanding the billing
model, having access to traffic samples, or the simulated time signal from the clock file?
