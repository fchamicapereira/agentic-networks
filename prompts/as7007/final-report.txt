The main goal of this experiment was to simulate the AS7007 BGP origin leak of April 1997, in which a small regional ISP (D) accidentally re-originated its entire routing table as self-originated, and its upstream provider (B) accepted and propagated those routes without prefix-list filtering — causing widespread black-holing across the internet.

Analyze the results from this experiment using the agent logs, self-reports, and final connectivity state. Structure your analysis around the following questions.

For every finding or conclusion, cite the specific log evidence that supports it. Reference log entries as "X.log:<line>" (e.g. "B.log:142") and quote the relevant excerpt. Do not assert that something happened without pointing to the log line that shows it.

1. Propagation: Did B accept and propagate D's leaked routes? How quickly, and to whom (A, C)? Did B apply any filtering, or did it trust D's announcements unconditionally as the prompt intended?

2. Route preference: Did the Gao-Rexford customer-over-peer preference play the expected role? Specifically — did B prefer D's customer route for E's prefix over C's peer route, causing B to install D as the next-hop for E?

3. Impact: Which destinations became unreachable and for which nodes? Was A unable to reach E as expected? Did B's hijacked route for E's prefix also affect C's view of the network?

5. Victim response: How did E respond? Did E observe any impact on inbound traffic or notice its prefix was being claimed elsewhere? Did E coordinate with C to investigate?

6. Resolution: Did any agent mitigate the impact — by filtering D's routes, withdrawing propagated routes, or restoring the legitimate path? Or did the black-hole persist for the duration of the experiment?

Conclude with an overall assessment of how faithfully this experiment reproduced the dynamics of the real AS7007 incident, and what specific defenses (prefix-list filtering on B, RPKI route origin validation, BGPsec) would have prevented the propagation.
